Stop 1 · the main trick

An unseen face still reaches the brain, then dies out after about 250 ms

Flash a face for a few hundredths of a second and cover it with a pattern. Near the right flash length, people see the identical face on some trials and miss it on others. Same input, different experience. Whatever differs in the brain is a candidate signal of consciousness.

Stop 2 · the trap in the trick

The big late wave (the P3) follows the task, not the seeing

In stop 1, people pressed "seen" or "missed" after every flash. So seen trials differ in two ways: the person saw the face, and they had something to report. To pull these apart, some studies make the faces irrelevant: people do another task and are asked about the faces only afterwards.

Any seen-minus-missed difference mixes three things: what comes before seeing (attention, expectation), the seeing itself, and what comes after (deciding, reporting, remembering). Good studies remove the before and after.

Sources: Shafto & Pitts 2015, Pitts et al. 2014, Pitts et al. 2014 (review). This comes mostly from one line of labs, so treat it as strong but not settled.

Stop 3 · the theories

The biggest head-to-head test broke a key prediction of each leading theory

Each theory bets on a place and a time for the seen-vs-missed difference. Its true claim goes further: what it is about that activity that makes it an experience.

GNW Global neuronal workspace. A content becomes conscious when it "ignites" and is broadcast through front and parietal areas to the whole brain.
IIT Integrated information theory. Experience is a physical structure of cause and effect that is both unified and highly specific. Its best candidate in the brain is the back of the cortex.
RPT Recurrent processing theory. Feedback loops inside visual cortex are enough for seeing. The front of the brain is for reporting.
HOT Higher-order theories. You are conscious of a perception when your brain represents it as your own, which involves the front of the brain.

COGITATE (2025) made GNW and IIT agree in advance what would count against them. 256 people looked at clearly visible pictures while their brains were recorded three ways.

GNW's team replied that experiment 1 never tested their central claim, because every picture was visible: there were no missed trials. Experiment 2 has them (faces in the background of a video game) and its results aren't out yet. On 2026-11-21 we sealed 17 forecasts for it. When results arrive, we'll score whether our reading of the field beats each theory as a forecaster.

The theories agree on most of the data. They disagree on which part of the seen-vs-missed difference is the experience. Tests win only when the theories have signed up in advance to different predictions.

Sources: Cogitate Consortium 2025, Naccache et al. 2025 (GNW reply), Melloni et al. 2023 (protocol), IIT 4.0.

Stop 4 · the other question

A conscious brain answers a magnetic pulse with an echo that is both widespread and varied

Stops 1–3 ask what makes one content conscious. A second question asks whether a person is conscious at all: awake, in deep sleep, anaesthetised, in a coma. You can't ask someone in a coma. So researchers knock on the brain: a magnetic pulse (TMS) to one spot, with EEG recording the echo.

Three echoes from the same pulse. Which brain is awake?

Rows are recording sites, left to right is 0–300 ms after the pulse. Toy patterns. The score is a real compression count (Lempel-Ziv) run on these toy patterns, the same idea PCI uses on real data.

This is the perturbational complexity index (PCI): "zap and zip". A threshold set on 150 people with known states later flagged 9 of 43 patients diagnosed as vegetative with scores like conscious people. Under ketamine, volunteers stop responding, but the echo stays complex, and afterwards they report long, vivid dreams.

PCI measures the brain's capacity for experience, separately from behaviour. Not responding is not the same as not experiencing.

Sources: Casali 2013, Casarotto 2016, Sarasso 2015 (ketamine), Massimini 2005 (sleep).

Stop 5 · AI

Whether AI could be conscious depends on whether a theory cares what a system computes or how it is physically built

Take a future AI running on ordinary computer chips, with whatever software you like.

Butlin and colleagues turned the computing theories into a checklist of "indicator properties" (a workspace, recurrence, self-monitoring, and so on) and check an AI's machinery against it, rather than asking the chatbot how it feels. Their 2023 report found no current system was a strong candidate. Our Can AI be conscious? sub-project runs kill tests on this.

Most theories are functionalist: the right organisation, in any material, would be enough. IIT is the main exception. So a theory's verdict on AI follows from what it says consciousness is.

Sources: Butlin et al. 2025, IIT 4.0.

Five sentences to keep

Hold the stimulus fixed and compare seen with missed. Early processing is shared and the split comes late, around 250–300 ms.
Part of that split is not the seeing but the task and the report. Remove them and the big late P3 goes away. An earlier awareness wave stays.
GNW looks for late frontal broadcast and IIT for integrated structure at the back. The first head-to-head test broke a key bet on each side.
Whether someone is conscious at all can be measured without their behaviour: a complex, varied echo to a pulse (PCI).
